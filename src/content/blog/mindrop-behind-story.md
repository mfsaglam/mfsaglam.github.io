A while ago, I removed almost every app icon from my iPhone's Home Screen.

I wasn't trying to make my phone look minimal. I just wanted to have more control over what I see every time I pick it up.

Instead of pages full of app icons, I started filling my Home Screen with widgets. Reminders, calendar events, notes from Obsidian, books I'm reading, and lists I want to keep an eye on.

The difference was surprisingly noticeable.

My phone was no longer showing me a collection of apps I could open. It was showing me information I actually cared about.

Of course, removing app icons meant I needed another way to open my apps.

That's when Spotlight became part of my daily routine.

## Getting Used to Spotlight

At first, I used Spotlight just to launch apps.

But the more I used it, the more I realized how useful it was. I started searching for emails, looking things up on the web, and finding information without opening the corresponding apps first.

Eventually, searching became more natural than navigating.

And somewhere along the way, I noticed something interesting.

Sometimes, I didn't really want to open an app at all. I just wanted to find a tiny piece of information.

Maybe a locker code. A number someone asked me to remember. Or something my wife or one of my kids told me that I knew I'd probably forget five minutes later.

Sure, I could save these things in Apple Notes.

But that felt like too much effort for something so small.

I'd have to open Notes, create or find a note, type the information, and later search through my notes to find it again.

I wanted something simpler.

Just save a piece of information, give it a keyword, and forget about it until I need it.

## Building Something for Myself

So I built a small iOS app.

The idea was simple: store a keyword and its corresponding value, then make that information accessible through Spotlight.

No complicated organization. No folders. No unnecessary features.

Just something I could save quickly and find even faster.

The first version was pretty terrible, at least visually.

But it worked.

And since I was the only person using it, I didn't really care how it looked. I built it, installed it on my iPhone, and went on with my life.

I kept using that little app for almost a year.

There was no App Store release, no marketing, and no plan to turn it into a product.

It was simply a tool I had made for myself.

## Maybe It's Not Just Me

Things changed when I realized my wife had a similar need.

That made me reconsider the app.

Maybe this wasn't just my strange way of organizing information. Maybe other people also had little things they wanted to remember without turning them into full-blown notes.

I started thinking about publishing it on the App Store.

Of course, the original interface wasn't something I felt comfortable releasing. It had been designed for exactly one user: me.

So I spent some time cleaning things up, improving the interface, and making the experience a little more thoughtful.

The goal, however, stayed the same.

I didn't want to build another note-taking app.

I just wanted to make it easier to remember the small things.

## From My Phone to the App Store

That little app is now available on the App Store as **MindDrop**.

You can find MindDrop on [its website](https://mfsaglam.github.io/mindrop/), which also links to the App Store.

It's still a small application, and that's intentional.

I like the idea that not every app needs to solve a massive problem, have dozens of features, or become the next big thing.

Sometimes, a tiny inconvenience in your everyday life is enough of a reason to build something.

And sometimes, the best way to find out whether an idea is useful is simply to live with it for a while.

That's how this one started.
